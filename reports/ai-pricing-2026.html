Algorithmic Pricing AI Policy Blog Post November 2026  |  Cara Sherpa

AI Pricing for Consumers

Cara Sherpa is a graduate research assistant with the Center for Analytics and Innovation with Data (CAID) at the University of Denver's Daniels College of Business. In this invited post, Cara uses data from CAID's State AI Policy Tracker to examine how state legislatures are approaching algorithmic pricing and what proposed bills reveal about the data that shapes the prices consumers pay.

Imagine finding out that you paid more for the same product simply because an algorithm decided you were willing to. I kept returning to a simple question while looking over state legislation, should two people be charged different prices for the same thing because an algorithm thinks one of them will pay more? Businesses have long adjusted prices based on factors like demand, location, or the cost of providing a service. However, AI systems can make those decisions using much more personal information. Browsing history, past purchases, location, financial circumstances could all influence what a consumer is offered. This makes algorithmic pricing an important consumer-protection issue because the concern is not only how a price is calculated, but also what information was used to calculate it.

Louisiana HB 471, introduced in 2026, addressed this concern directly. The bill would have prohibited surveillance-based price discrimination, which it defined as using surveillance data to set individualized prices. That data could include a person's behavior, biometric information, browsing history, purchase history, location, financial circumstances, and personal characteristics. The proposal wouldn't have prohibited every difference in price. It included exceptions for differences based on the actual cost of providing a product or service, discounts offered to consumers on equal terms, and certain risk-related insurance decisions. This distinction matters. The bill wasn't arguing that every personalized price is automatically unfair. Instead, it questioned whether businesses should be allowed to use extensive personal data to determine how much an individual consumer might be willing to pay. Louisiana HB 471 remained pending in the House Committee on Commerce and didn't become law.

Maryland HB 434 approached algorithmic pricing from a different direction. Rather than focusing on information collected about an individual consumer, the bill addressed the use of nonpublic competitor data in the rental market. It would have prohibited landlords from relying on algorithmic devices that used, incorporated, or were trained with private competitor information to determine rent, lease-renewal terms, occupancy levels, or other lease conditions. That proposal highlights another possible harm. An algorithm may not need to know everything about a particular renter to affect what that person pays. It could instead combine private information from multiple landlords in ways that reduce meaningful competition and push rents in the same direction. The bill would have treated a violation as an unfair, abusive, or deceptive trade practice, but Maryland HB 434 didn't advance beyond the legislative process.

What stood out to me is that these bills address two different sides of the same problem. Louisiana HB 471 asked whether a pricing system knows too much about the consumer. Maryland HB 434 asked whether it knows too much about competitors. In both situations, the person paying the price may have no idea what information shaped the amount they were offered. This is also where disclosure and human review begin to show their limits. Telling someone that an algorithm helped set a price doesn't explain which data it used or whether that data should have been used in the first place. Having a person approve the final price may not solve the problem either if that person is relying on a system built around surveillance.

Neither proposal became law, so these bills reveal more about the direction of the policy debate than the protections consumers currently have. Still, they raise questions that lawmakers will increasingly have to confront. Consumers may need more than notice that AI is involved. They may also need limits on the information businesses can use, a way to challenge questionable decisions, and someone who can be held responsible when algorithmic pricing causes harm.

Across these bills, one point has become clear to me, AI consumer protection can't be reduced to a warning label or a human signature. Disclosure matters, and human review matters, but so do the data going into a system and the consequences coming out of it. AI may make pricing faster or more efficient, but efficiency shouldn't mean that consumers quietly pay more because an algorithm knows their habits or lack of alternatives.
